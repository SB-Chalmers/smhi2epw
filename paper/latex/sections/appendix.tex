\section{Weather file details}\label{app:A}
\setcounter{figure}{0}\setcounter{table}{0}
\renewcommand{\thefigure}{A\arabic{figure}}
\renewcommand{\thetable}{A\arabic{table}}

The finished file must have every hour in order, all values within physical limits, a dew point no higher than the air temperature, solar values that are not negative and that add up, and no radiation in dark hours. Sudden jumps where one data source meets another are flagged, but real extreme weather is never removed.

ERA5 values are downloaded through the Open-Meteo archive service \citep{zippenfenig2024}, not directly from ECMWF. The ERA5 model is asked for by name, because the service otherwise mixes several models, and the station height is passed, so that the service corrects temperature for the height difference. Station values that SMHI marks as checked and approved (quality code G) or as suspect or only roughly checked (code Y) are used \citep{smhi2026a}. A solar station with a full year of data is preferred, but one with gaps can be used.

All data are first placed on an hourly grid in universal time, then shifted to fixed local standard time with no summer time, as EnergyPlus expects. Dew point and long-wave sky radiation are calculated from the final weather values. Total cloud cover is used to estimate sky radiation, because SMHI does not report the thicker ``opaque'' cloud that the EPW format also asks for.

EPW files give the average radiation over the hour before each time stamp, so STRÅNG's instant values are averaged in pairs. ERA5 values are already hourly averages and are used as they are. When only total radiation on a flat surface is known, it is split into direct and diffuse parts with the Erbs--Driesse method \citep{erbs1982,driesse2024} in the pvlib library \citep{anderson2023}. The position of the sun is calculated with the NREL solar position algorithm \citep{reda2004}, checked every five minutes so that hours that are only partly sunlit are kept. Radiation is set to zero in hours that are fully dark, and the direct and diffuse parts always add up to the total.

Each weather file comes with a record file. It lists the settings, the software version, the nearby stations used and their test errors, the ERA5 grid point used, and the share of each variable that came from measurements, the station's own record, nearby stations or ERA5. Each file is labelled as measured, partly filled or model-only.

To see how the stepwise method fills real records, we built weather files with a two-day switch for 60 station-years across four latitude bands and three levels of missing data. All 60 were completed. Linear interpolation supplied 6.9\% of filled values, nearby stations 7.5\%, daily patterns 2.0\% and ERA5 83.6\% (Figure~\ref{fig:A1}), as most missing hours lie in gaps longer than 48 hours. Without the switch, nearby stations would have supplied 36\% and ERA5 55\%. In station-years complete enough to be a reference, nearby stations supplied 35\% of filled values and ERA5 none. ERA5 supplied 59\% of filled air temperature values and 74--94\% of the other variables.

\textbf{How strict the nearby-station check should be.} We repeated the scoring with a temperature limit of 2~°C or 1.5~°C instead of 3~°C, giving each newly rejected case the ERA5 fill (an approximation, as the real method would try daily patterns first). Of the cases that used a nearby station for temperature, a 2~°C limit rejected about a quarter and a 1.5~°C limit about two in five. For 48-hour gaps, the stricter limits cut the worst cooling error from 8.7\% to 4.9\% of annual cooling, and at 1.5~°C the worst regret for the cooling peak fell from 20\% to 16\%, the same as with a one-day switch. For 12-hour gaps they made the worst case worse (worst regret 12\% to 16\%), as a good nearby station beats ERA5 over short gaps. The limit and the switch point act on the same few weak cases, each trading a little typical accuracy for a smaller worst case. We kept 3~°C, with the switch point as the main control.

\textbf{Order of the local steps.} Putting the checked nearby station before daily patterns, as the stepwise method does, reduced the 48-hour heating error from 0.61\% to 0.40\% and the cooling error from 2.4\% to 1.3\%, compared with the stepwise method with patterns first. For 12-hour gaps, it gave a slightly smaller heating error (0.11\% against 0.13\%) but a larger cooling error (1.1\% against 0.6\%). The larger cooling error comes from the 8\% of 12-hour gaps in which temperature and humidity came from different sources at the same hours, one from a nearby station and the other from daily patterns. Without these files the stepwise method's cooling error was 0.43\%, against 0.39\% for ERA5 only. For 3-hour gaps the two orders gave the same results, because linear interpolation comes first in both.

\textbf{Check of the STRÅNG step.} We compared STRÅNG, without correction, and ERA5 with measured total radiation in the 12 region-years that have a radiation station within 50~km of the weather station (0.6--45~km), over daylight hours only (measured radiation above 5~W/m², about 4,400--4,500 hours per region-year). The mean absolute error was 47~W/m² for STRÅNG and 48~W/m² for ERA5, averaged over the 12 region-years, and STRÅNG was better in 6 of them. Where the radiation station sits next to the weather station (Luleå, 0.6~km), STRÅNG was clearly better: 32--37~W/m² against 39--41~W/m².
